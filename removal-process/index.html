THE REMOVAL PROCESS

How to Hold Any Office Accountable, Starting in Your Own Area

Every office in America has a door. Removal is not a wish or a hashtag — it is a process, with a named door, a named threshold, and paperwork real people file every year. Some doors citizens open themselves; others are held by legislatures, where citizens build the record and pressure that moves them. Knowing which door is real is the whole game: half of all failed efforts aim at a door that doesn’t exist. This page walks through each one so you can start in your own area. No outcome is promised. But the process is real, and it belongs to you.

FIND YOUR DOOR

Members of Congress (U.S. House and Senate)

There is no citizen recall of a member of Congress. The Constitution fixes House terms at two years and Senate terms at six, and reserves expulsion to a member’s own chamber — settled in Committee to Recall Robert Menendez v. Wells (2010), applying U.S. Term Limits v. Thornton (1995). Members are not impeachable either (settled in 1797).

The real doors:

  • Expulsion — a member’s own chamber, by two-thirds vote. Rare (21 in U.S. history, most of them Civil War cases), but real: Rep. George Santos was expelled on December 1, 2023, by 311–114, after a House Ethics Committee report documented fraud and misuse of campaign funds — the report is what moved the votes.
  • Resignation under pressure — the door that opens most often. Sen. Bob Packwood resigned in 1995 the day after the Ethics Committee voted unanimously to expel him.
  • Primary defeat — for a member in a safe seat, the primary is the removal process. House Majority Leader Eric Cantor lost his primary in 2014.

Your levers: document the record, file ethics complaints (both chambers accept public information), make criminal referrals where facts support them, and organize for the primary.

The President and Executive Officers

  • Impeachment and conviction. The House impeaches by simple majority; the Senate convicts by two-thirds of Senators present, and conviction means removal. Three presidents have been impeached (Johnson, Clinton, Trump twice); none was ever convicted, and Nixon resigned in 1974 when conviction became certain. Citizens have no direct role; the lever is sustained, documented pressure on House members.
  • The 25th Amendment is not a removal tool for wrongdoing. It covers inability to serve: Section 3 is a temporary, voluntary transfer (used for medical procedures); Section 4 — Vice President plus a majority of the cabinet declaring inability — has never been used.

Federal Judges

Federal judges serve for life, and there is exactly one removal door: impeachment — House majority, Senate two-thirds. Only eight federal judges have ever been convicted and removed (John Pickering in 1804 through G. Thomas Porteous in 2010), every one for corruption, crime, or collapse of fitness — never a ruling people disliked.

What citizens can do: under the Judicial Conduct and Disability Act (28 U.S.C. §§ 351–364), any person may file a conduct complaint about a federal judge with the clerk of the court of appeals. A judicial council can investigate, reprimand, and ask a judge to retire — but it cannot remove an Article III judge. The complaint is a paper-trail tool.

State Officials (Governors, Legislators, Attorneys General)

Recall exists in 19 states plus the District of Columbia: Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Georgia, Idaho, Illinois, Kansas, Louisiana, Michigan, Minnesota, Montana, Nevada, New Jersey, North Dakota, Oregon, Rhode Island, Washington, Wisconsin, and DC. If your state is on it, voters can remove a state official mid-term.

  • In most recall states, no reason is required. Eight states require specific grounds (Alaska: lack of fitness, incompetence, neglect of duty, corruption).
  • Signature thresholds are a percentage of the votes cast in the last election for that office: California requires 12% for statewide office, with 160 days to gather; Alaska, Arizona, and Colorado require 25%; DC requires 10% of registered voters; Illinois allows recall of the governor only (15%, with bipartisan legislative support first).
  • Many states bar filing in the first or last months of a term. Before planning, read your state’s current table (NCSL’s “Recall of State Officials” and Ballotpedia’s signature table are the standing references).

In 2003, California organizers needed 897,156 valid signatures to recall Gov. Gray Davis; they turned in about 1.6 million, and voters removed him 55%–45%. Wisconsin’s Gov. Walker survived his recall in 2012 — qualifying is not the same as winning.

Texas has no recall of state officials at all — not the governor, not legislators, not the attorney general. The Texas door is impeachment: the House impeaches by majority, the Senate convicts by two-thirds of senators present, and the official is suspended while the case is pending. In 2023 the Texas House impeached Attorney General Ken Paxton 121–23; the Senate acquitted him on all 16 articles tried, because no article reached the 21 votes required. The House’s record got the impeachment; the Senate threshold decided it. If your state is neither Texas nor on the recall list, impeachment by your state legislature is likely your door — check your state constitution.

Local Officials (Mayors, Council Members, County Officers)

In Texas, recall exists only where a city charter creates it. Home-rule cities (population 5,000+) write their own charters, and most include recall. Thresholds are charter-specific — never guess yours: Fredericksburg and Wolfforth both set it at 10% (of registered voters; of those registered at the last general election); other charters run 25–30% of votes cast. Counties have no recall — but county officers can be removed by petition and trial in district court under Local Government Code chapter 87: a citizen files, a jury decides, on grounds of incompetency, official misconduct, or intoxication.

To find your city’s exact rule, anywhere in the country: (1) Get the charter — city website, the City Secretary or Clerk’s office, or the Municode library online. (2) Find the article titled “Recall.” (3) Pull four numbers: who can be recalled, the signature formula and its base (registered voters vs. votes cast), the window, and any grace period. (4) Confirm voter figures with your county elections office — that denominator decides the whole campaign.

Law-Enforcement Officers

An officer holds two things: a job with one agency, and a license from the state. Firing without touching the license is how problem officers resurface one county over — the license is the removal that follows them.

In Texas, the licensing body is TCOLE — the Texas Commission on Law Enforcement. Anyone may file a complaint with TCOLE, but know its jurisdiction: licensing, training, and certification violations, and license holders convicted of — or placed on deferred adjudication for — a covered criminal offense. It does not investigate rudeness or botched investigations; those belong to the employing agency. TCOLE can reprimand, suspend, or revoke a license; a felony conviction means revocation. A termination for misconduct is recorded on the agency’s separation report (the F-5) as a dishonorable discharge, and since 2023 reforms (SB 1445), Texas revocations go to a national decertification database — so a revocation follows the officer across state lines.

Run the doors in this order:

  1. Internal affairs, in writing. Texas law (Government Code §614.022) requires a formal complaint against an officer to be in writing and signed before the agency must act on it. Be specific: date, place, badge or name, what happened.
  2. The governing body if internal affairs stalls — city manager or council, school board, or commissioners court.
  3. Criminal misconduct goes to the county or district attorney, and/or the Texas Rangers. A conviction then feeds the licensing process automatically.
  4. The written record is the whole strategy — documented, sustained findings survive appeals; hallway complaints don’t.

Outside Texas the shape is the same: every state has an officer licensing or standards body (often called POST). Find yours, learn its jurisdiction, and start with a written, signed complaint to the agency.

ORGANIZE: HOW A REMOVAL CAMPAIGN IS ACTUALLY BUILT

  1. Name the target and the door. Identify the exact office and which mechanism above reaches it. A petition aimed at a door that doesn’t exist is dead paper.
  2. Count the need before filing anything. Get the denominator from the elections office — votes cast for the office last election, or registered voters, whichever your rule uses — and compute the exact requirement. Then plan to gather 130–150% of it: invalidation rates of 20–30% are normal, and sink campaigns that gathered the bare minimum.
  3. Form the committee before money moves. Organize as a named committee with a treasurer, a separate bank account, and written records. In Texas, you must appoint a campaign treasurer and register with the Texas Ethics Commission before accepting or spending a dollar; other states have their own version — find it first.
  4. Draft the petition carefully. It is a legal document: the statement of grounds where required, the official’s name and office exactly as on the ballot, and signer lines carrying everything your jurisdiction requires (printed name, signature, address, date of birth or voter registration number, date signed). Texas Election Code chapter 277 sets the baseline in Texas. Many jurisdictions require the form approved or filed before circulation begins — the clock starts there.
  5. Gather signatures lawfully. Signers must be registered voters of the jurisdiction — and of the district, for district offices; a signature from outside the line is void. Paid circulators are lawful in most states (Meyer v. Grant, 1988), though some jurisdictions restrict who may circulate or ban pay-per-signature — check your rule. Never sign for another person or backdate — forgery is a crime everywhere and poisons the filing.
  6. Mind the clock, then file. Circulation windows are hard deadlines (160 days in California statewide, 60 in Colorado, 120 in Arizona), and grace periods can void an early filing — work backward from the election date. The elections official verifies signatures, and the official usually gets a window to resign; some do. If the election is called, you are now running a real election campaign, with fundraising rules and reporting deadlines, and in some states the replacement sits on the same ballot.
  7. Avoid the pitfalls that kill petitions: the wrong denominator; unregistered or out-of-district signers; missing circulator affidavits; circulating before the form was approved; a deadline missed by a day; a grounds statement that doesn’t match your state’s required categories; money commingled without a registered committee. All are preventable at steps 3 and 4. And step zero, always: thresholds change, so re-verify your number against the current statute or charter before filing.
